# Field foundations: from a seed to a harvest

This guide supports the AlmanacPhi web app. Its advice is general. Local weather, soil type, crop cultivar, land practices, and extension guidance take priority.

## 1. Observe the site

- **Light:** Observe 6–8 hours of direct sun for typical fruiting crops, but note that trees, fences, or buildings alter actual exposure. Record where morning and afternoon shadows fall.
- **Drainage:** After rain, look for long-lasting puddles. Avoid compaction and waterlogging. Raised beds or containers can help when existing soil drains poorly.
- **Soil safety:** Where land has a history of industrial use, mining, demolition, roadsides, or painted structures, check for contamination (especially lead) before growing edible crops.
- **Soil test:** Use a lab or local extension service for pH and nutrient recommendations. Apply fertilizer based on the results instead of guessing.

## 2. Pick the season, then the crop

**Cool-season:** Lettuce, spinach, kale, broccoli, peas, carrots, beets, radishes, onions, potatoes and garlic generally prefer cooler weather, though hardiness and sowing methods differ.

**Warm-season:** Tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, corn, beans, basil, summer squash, and sunflowers generally need frost-free conditions and warmer soil. A warm day after a cold night does not mean the soil is ready.

**Microclimates:** Every yard has local temperature differences. Frost pockets occur on low ground. Walls, windbreaks, paving, tree shade, slope, nearby water, and elevation influence plant performance. Average dates are not safety guarantees.

## 3. Sow correctly

1. Read the seed packet for cultivar-specific temperature, depth, spacing, and days to maturity.
2. Moisten the growing medium before sowing. Aim for damp, not saturated soil.
3. Plant to the recommended depth; tiny seeds are often shallow, larger seeds deeper.
4. Keep the seed zone consistently moist through germination.
5. Thin crowded seedlings early, reducing competition. For roots like carrots, direct sow instead of transplanting.
6. For transplants, provide strong light indoors and gradually harden off young plants before moving them outside.
7. Avoid planting in soil that is too wet to work or too cold for your species.

## 4. Keep plants healthy

- **Water:** Check soil moisture a few inches down before watering. Water needs vary by root depth, soil, plant size, temperature, wind, and rainfall. Deep, appropriately spaced watering often works better than shallow daily sprinkles.
- **Mulch:** Use clean organic mulch to moderate temperature and retain moisture. Keep it clear of stems and crowns to avoid rot.
- **Nutrients:** Too much nitrogen can make leafy plants at the expense of fruit or roots. Compost helps soil structure but does not replace a soil test.
- **Pests:** Identify the organism and extent of damage before intervening. Encourage biodiversity and beneficial insects. Hand removal, barriers, sanitation, and crop rotation are often useful first steps.
- **Pollination:** Beans, tomatoes, corn, squash, and cucumbers have different pollination needs. Bees and other pollinators matter for many fruit crops; sweet corn is wind-pollinated and grows best in blocks.

## 5. Read the seasons

- **Winter:** Plan beds, service tools, protect vulnerable plants, and avoid working saturated soil.
- **Spring:** Sow hardy crops early only when soil is workable; start warm crops under protection; harden off transplants.
- **Summer:** Track watering and heat stress, harvest frequently, monitor pests, and plan fall greens early enough.
- **Autumn:** Harvest warm crops before killing frost, sow compatible cool crops, improve soil, and cover bare beds where appropriate.

Astronomical seasons are not agricultural boundaries. Weather and the growing season are local.

## 6. Sun and Moon

Day length influences photosynthesis and some plant developmental cues (including day-length-dependent bulbing in onions). The app provides a **rough solar geometry calculation**, not hourly shade maps.

The Moon's phases repeat approximately every 29.53 days. Lunar gardening traditions can be meaningful parts of culture and observation, but evidence is currently insufficient to claim that waxing or waning phases directly improve crop yield. Never miss a suitable weather/soil planting window to follow an unproven calendar rule.

## 7. Record your results

For every planting, note the crop variety, supplier/seed lot, actual sowing date, last frost, soil temperature if known, irrigation pattern, shade, pests, and harvest. Compare results across *multiple* plantings and seasons before drawing conclusions. A small rigorous local record is more useful than a universal claim.
